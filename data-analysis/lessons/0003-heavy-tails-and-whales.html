Heavy Tails & Whales

The mean is a democracy where dollars vote instead of orders — and one rich voter can swing any election.

The win By the end (~10 min): you can tell when an average is lying, back out the whale that moved it, and choose the right defense (median, pooling, capping) — knowing what each one trades away.

01Two shapes of randomness

Lesson 0002's math quietly assumed data that clusters symmetrically around its mean — bell-shaped, like conversion counts. Money is not shaped like that. Order values are heavy-tailed: most orders are modest, but rare huge ones carry a big share of the total dollars.

bell (counts, rates):          heavy tail (order values, latencies):

        ▄█▄                        █▄
       █████                       ███▄
      ███████                      █████▄▄
     █████████                     ████████▄▄▄___________
   ──────┼──────              ──────┼────────────────▲───
        mean                      median            whales live here
  mean ≈ median ✓                 mean is PULLED RIGHT by the tail

A standard model for money-like data: the log-normal — X ~ LogNormal(μ, σ) just means log X is bell-shaped (the TS decoder is literally Math.exp(mu + sigma * normalDraw())). Drag the tail wider and watch the mean get pulled away from the pinned median — at σ near zero it's a symmetric bell and they agree; at realistic order-value spread they've parted company:

With a median order of $85 and realistic spread (σ ≈ 0.8), the mean order is ~$117 and 1 in 100 orders exceeds $550. The mean and the typical order stopped being the same thing.

02Why the mean breaks: every dollar votes

Look at the mean's definition — each observation enters at weight 1/n:

x̄ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ        one order's dollars enter the average at weight 1/n
Average Order Value = every order's full dollar amount ÷ order count — no vote is capped

With n = 10 orders, a single $1,500 order contributes $150 to AOV all by itself — more than a typical order's entire value. The mean has a breakdown point of 0%: one sufficiently extreme point moves it arbitrarily far.

Now the real case. Aug 24 desktop control: 10 orders, $2,527.34 demand, AOV $252.73. Back the whale out yourself: if the other 9 orders averaged ~$110 (the test arm's AOV that day), what was order #10?

reveal the whale 
whale = 2,527.34 − 9 × 110 ≈ $1,537
one order carried 61% of the arm's entire daily revenue

That single basket produced the day's "−25% RPV" headline. No behavior changed anywhere — one shopper had a big cart on the control side. This arithmetic (total − n×typical) is a 30-second check worth running on any suspicious AOV day.

03The median: where orders vote, not dollars

median = the middle value: half the orders above, half below
breakdown point 50% — you must corrupt half the data to move it arbitrarily; the mean needs one point

The median answers "what does a typical order look like?" and simply cannot see whale magnitude — a $1,537 order counts exactly as much as a $130 one. This is why our hero-paint metrics are p50s (latency is heavy-tailed too), and why "median order value" is the honest companion to AOV. The trade: the median changes the question. Finance is paid in sums, and sums are whale-inclusive — a metric that ignores whales ignores real revenue. Robust and complete are different virtues; carry both numbers.

04The defense toolbox

DefenseWhat it doesWhat it trades
Pool longer windowsgrows n so each whale's weight (1/n) shrinksslower answers; still mean-based
Median (p50)immune to whale magnitudeanswers "typical", not "total dollars"
Cap / winsorizeclip order values at e.g. the 99th percentile before averagingsmall deliberate bias, big variance cut — the industry standard for revenue metrics (Kohavi)
Show n beside the metrican AOV without its order count is uninterpretablenothing — always do this
Trap — the √n law degrades here Lesson 0002's SE math still holds for heavy tails, but the per-order variance it divides by is enormous (order values swing ~±95% each), so convergence is slow and the √-count rule (±1/√O) becomes a floor, not an estimate. That's why the protocol calls its AOV bands "optimistic."

06Check yourself

How you'd say it to a stakeholder Average order value is like average net worth in a bar: the moment one billionaire walks in, the "average patron" is worth $50 million, and nothing about the bar changed. On a ten-order day, one big basket is that billionaire. That's why I quote the typical order and the month's total — the daily average is the one number that can't be trusted alone.
