Reading While Running

Why pivoting on daily numbers always feels like it works — and the two-lane protocol that keeps a moving experiment analyzable.

The win By the end (~10 min): you can recognize the tampering loop in a meeting as it happens, explain why the "fix" was guaranteed to look successful, and name what a legal daily action actually is.

01The loop

bad daily number ──▶ pivot (model swap, rollback) ──▶ number recovers ──▶ "the fix worked!"
       ▲                                                                        │
       └────────────────── team trusts the loop a little more ◀────────────────┘

Every arrow in that loop is real except the causal one. The recovery was going to happen anyway — and the next section proves it with one line of probability.

02Regression to the mean, quantified

If days are independent draws around a stable truth, then no matter what today looked like, tomorrow's expectation is just the mean:

E[Xtomorrow | Xtoday = terrible] = μ
tomorrow's expected value, given ANY today = the process mean — extremes don't propagate

So intervene on a 2σ-bad day and ask: what's the chance tomorrow beats today? Tomorrow is a fresh draw; it beats a 2σ-low value whenever it lands above μ−2σ, which the bell curve (Φ) prices at:

P(Xnext > μ − 2σ) = Φ(2) ≈ 97.7%
a placebo shipped on a very bad day "works" about 98% of the time

See it on the bell itself — drag the slider to how bad "today" was (in noise units) and watch the green area: everything above today's mark is a tomorrow that "proves the fix worked":

That is the engine of the loop: any action taken at a trough gets credited by arithmetic. Ship a model change, a rollback, or a rubber duck on the worst day of the month, and the metric improves ~98% of the time. Deming demonstrated the harm side too (the funnel experiment): reacting to individual outcomes from a stable process doesn't just fail — your adjustments become a new variance source stacked on the old one. 03Peeking: checking daily has a price even without pivots

Suppose you never pivot — you just check the dashboard daily and pounce when |z| ≥ 2. The 5% false-alarm guarantee belongs to one look; taking the maximum over many looks is a different (much easier) bar for noise to clear:

one pre-committed look:  P(false alarm) = 5%
peek continuously & stop at the first |z| ≥ 2:  P ≈ 26%
a quarter of A/A tests "find a winner" under continuous peeking (Evan Miller)

The legal versions: fixed pre-scheduled readouts (our R1), or sequential bounds that spend the 5% across looks (what platforms like Statsig implement). What's never legal: deciding the window after seeing the data — that's the Texas sharpshooter, painting the target around the bullet holes.

04Tampering's second cost: the moving target

Beyond fake credit, every mid-test pivot damages the measurement itself, twice:

The blend problem. After pivots, "the test arm" is a time-varying sequence (UI-v1 + model-A, then model-B, …). The cumulative lift measures a blend that corresponds to no shippable configuration.

The trough-selection problem. Because pivots trigger on bad reads, the "before" period is systematically a trough — so before/after comparisons around any metric-triggered change are biased toward "it worked" even for a placebo (that's §02 again, wearing analysis clothes). Our own Aug 18 model change carries exactly this bias, which is why we leaned on the untouched device as a control before believing anything.

Plus the intervention log: every treatment change recorded before it ships, with its honest trigger ("planned work" vs "metric read") — the column that lets the team audit itself for tampering. The full pre-registered version for ATT-212 lives in the experiment protocol.

Trap — "no time to wait for significance" Detection time scales as 1/effect²: a catastrophe clears |z| ≥ 3 in a day or two; only small losses take weeks — and a loss too small for the statistics to see is too small for the daily dashboard to see. If there isn't enough data for confidence, there isn't enough data to know you're losing. (The revenue-exposure lever that doesn't destroy the test: cut the traffic split.)

06Check yourself

How you'd say it to a stakeholder Flight instructors used to swear that praise made pilots worse and scolding made them better — because a great landing is usually followed by a worse one, and a terrible landing by a better one, no matter what anyone says. Our dashboard works the same way: whatever we ship on the worst day will look like a fix. So we act fast on genuine breakage, and for everything else we decide on pre-scheduled pooled readouts, where the improvement has to beat arithmetic, not just yesterday.

07Go deeper

Primary source: Evan Miller, "How Not To Run an A/B Test" — the peeking essay; short, precise, and the origin of the 26% number. Pair with Kohavi et al.'s Twyman's-law chapter (Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments): "any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong."
