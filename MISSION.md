# Mission

## Why

Learn **probability** to improve real-world **decision-making** and build a deeper, more accurate understanding of how the world works. The goal is not to pass an exam — it is to internalize the highest-return concepts so they become instinctive tools for reasoning under uncertainty.

## Who

- Studied engineering; open to math notation as a challenge but **very rusty** — notation must be reintroduced from basics, never assumed
- Fluent in **TypeScript** — programming and CS analogies land well, especially for driving notation home (a symbol should map to a code concept already known)
- **Highly visual learner** — diagrams, simulations, and interactive widgets over walls of prose
- Wants analogies of all kinds, not just CS

## What Success Looks Like

1. **Intuition first**: can explain probability concepts in plain language and pictures before formulas
2. **Decision-making upgrades**: naturally applies expected value, Bayesian updating, base rates, and variance thinking to everyday choices
3. **A trophy shelf of formulas**: a growing reference collection of formulas earned through lessons — each one a compressed reminder of a concept truly understood
4. **Notation fluency, rebuilt from zero**: can read P(A|B), 𝔼[X], Σ, ∫ — each symbol introduced from scratch and anchored to a programming analogy

## Highest-Return Concepts (the curriculum spine)

Roughly ordered by expected return on decision-making:

1. Probability as a measure of belief; sample spaces and events
2. Conditional probability & Bayes' theorem (base-rate thinking)
3. Expected value & thinking in bets
4. Distributions (what shape is your uncertainty?)
5. Variance, risk, and the danger of averages
6. Law of large numbers vs. gambler's fallacy
7. Independence, correlation, and when intuition fails
8. Central limit theorem & why the normal distribution is everywhere
9. Monte Carlo simulation (the TypeScript-native superpower)
10. Common decision traps: conjunction fallacy, survivorship bias, regression to the mean

## Constraints & Preferences

- Lessons must be **interactive** — small programs, simulators, quizzes
- Visual over verbal wherever possible
- **Notation reintroduced from basics** — every new symbol gets: plain-language meaning → programming analogy → then the symbol itself
- Formulas are **trophies**, collected in reference docs after being earned
- Daily cadence ("do math errday") — lessons must be short enough to complete in one sitting
